01 / EPIGRAPH — JOHN LOCKE, 1690
Three basic operations
of the mind.

The acts of the mind, wherein it exerts its power over simple ideas, are chiefly these three:

1. Combining several simple ideas into one compound one—and thus all complex ideas are made.

2. Bringing two ideas, whether simple or complex, together, and setting them by one another so as to take a view of them at once, without uniting them into one—by which it gets all its ideas of relations.

3. Separating them from all other ideas that accompany them in their real existence; this is called abstraction, and thus all its general ideas are made.

— John Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690)

Locke is describing the three basic operations the mind performs on ideas:


Combination

Relation (comparison, to form ideas of relations)

Abstraction


Words written more than three hundred years ago land exactly on what we do in vvvv without noticing—wiring nodes, parameters and Pads together; laying several sets of logic side by side to compare them; distilling rules out of Patches that already exist.


Humans manage all of it by instinct.

02 / PROGRAM VISIBILITY
Program visibility
is also a degree of freedom.

Copying Patches, copying examples, dragging nodes and adjusting parameters is a fast way to get started—but the complexity of computation and of programs is not there to make the user's job harder. Don't be overwhelmed by a screen full of nodes and connections: program visibility is also a degree of freedom.

At different levels of abstraction, the things a person can do are different.

This is also why this series shares computational thinking rather than specific examples: AI can already do a great many things, and it is especially good at copying and migrating what already exists. How to open up more possibilities by understanding the computational process is a direction worth considering.

03 / START WITH THE FUNCTION
Everything begins with understanding the function.


Function.

y = f(x)

A function can be seen as the relationship between input, output, and a processing rule.

Input a pig, output a bowl of pork-trotter rice.

We abstract this processing into a function and name it “Kitchen.”

input → function → output

04 / THREE LAYERS

Primitives, encapsulation, black box.

Take a classic linear function as an example:

y = kx + b 

This is an expression built from vvvv's basic primitives and math library.

It means that everything can be reduced to a mathematical computational process.


This is the first expression, encapsulated as the function f (kx+b).

It keeps the control structure, but the rules of operation are hidden.


This is what we encounter most often; a structure like this can take the form of any program.

I named it the Pro version of kx+b: with a single operating parameter you get an approximate result. It is an opaque black box.

Don't think about k and b here: if you are not its designer and have no permission to go inside, that thinking loses its practical meaning.

(In fact it is an ultimate deluxe black box merely named kx+b_Pro: I put no computation inside it at all, only a value of 4, of type Integer32, on the output :p)


If we encapsulate a complex program completely and expose only one small interactive button, or keep just one dialog box, would it become a nearly perfect product?

The difference between the three layers is not how powerful they are,

it is how much of the computational process itself you can see,

and at which level you do the designing and defining.

05 / THE PROCESS ITSELF
The computational process itself
is an abstract thing.

In short, everything is about Computational Process.


The computational process itself is an abstract thing. It is not a node, not code, not anything visible on the screen. It is the abstraction of "data being processed by rules."


Process (an abstraction) → Operation → Data (an abstraction)

These processes are guided by a program (a pattern of rules)


The function y = f (x)  is one way of describing a process.

A node is a visual presentation of a process.

A Patch is an organizational structure for a process.

But the process itself—"one thing becoming another thing through some rule"—exists before any of these concrete forms.

06 / LINEAR & NONLINEAR
Linear is the most direct;
nonlinear is where chaos and emergence appear.
Mathematically, a linear function can be placed into the most direct control-and-response mapping. Beyond it there are nonlinear functions, which can produce chaos, emergence, and other behaviors.
The whole universe can be seen as one enormous nonlinear function, and the basis of AI (or deep learning / neural networks) is the layering of linear combinations and nonlinear activation functions.
—In vvvv, every function can be described as a node,
and as a runtime network made of nodes and links.


This is the second article in the series.

vvvv / VL, as a domain-specific language (DSL) in a visual live-programming environment, rests on operating principles that are no different in essence from those of any other language.

Nearly all mainstream programming languages today encapsulate functionality, including syntactic sugar, to make them easier to use and to lower cognitive complexity (though a badly designed program will still waste more computational resources).

But users of these environments almost never become involved in managing the underlying runtime or the compiler's evaluation process (how the iteration and recursion produced by a computational process unfolds). Complexity unfolds mainly at the level of algorithm design, program architecture, and the many kinds of real engineering requirements.
